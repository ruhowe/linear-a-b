# Kanta, Nakassis, Palaima, Perna (2025), *The Knossos Anetaki inscriptions*

Ariadne Suppl. 5: 27–43. Licence: **CC BY-NC-SA 4.0** (verified on the
journal page 2026-09-12; not CC BY — NonCommercial and ShareAlike both
apply, same family as DĀMOS and SigLA).

## What it claims

Reports two inscribed ivory objects from the Anetaki Ivory Repository
(Neopalatial Room 1, Knossos), parts of one composite object the excavator
calls a "religious sceptre": the **Ivory Ring (KN Zg 57)** and the **ivory
handle (KN Zg 58)**. No third Anetaki piece (no stone vessel) is edited.

- **Sign count**: the Ring carries ~119 signs total — "approximately 84...
  completely or partially preserved, and 35 in small traces or probably
  present in the space available." Every figure is hedged ("approximately",
  "about", "at least"), not a secured count. Called the longest known
  Linear A inscription, ahead of HT 123 (105 signs, but >40% numerals/
  dividers, leaving ~60 phonetic/logographic signs).
- **No numerals anywhere on the Ring** — the authors read this as
  diagnostic, placing it with stone libation vessels rather than accounting
  records. The handle, by contrast, is explicitly an accounting document
  (numerals, a sequence of six fraction signs) and is called "the first
  Linear A economic document coming from a cult building."
- **Word divisions**: none — there are no word-divider marks on the Ring.
  Individual incised **metopes** (rectangular framed spaces) do that job
  instead, at whatever granularity the incisor gave them (single sign,
  ligature, or sign-group); the authors state this explicitly ("the use of
  the metope feature on the Ring eliminates the need for... word-dividers").
- **Number of words**: not given. The authors count sign-groups per face
  (six on Face B, at least nine on Face C) but never spell out which signs
  compose them, and state outright that Face A has "no... phonetic sign
  groups" (individual signs/ligatures with vase ideograms instead).
- **Genre**: the Ring is read as a non-accounting ritual record — the
  iconography (12 animals, ~10 vessels) and script are treated as one
  integrated "message," possibly listing the elements of a ritual/feasting
  ceremony, compared to Hittite ritual-prescription texts. The handle is
  read as a genuine administrative/economic text.
- **Values-by-convention**: the article carries **no explicit caveat**
  about Linear A transliteration values being Linear B values applied by
  convention. It simply follows GORILA's own citation convention throughout
  (its footnote 7: "all Linear A documents and sign numbers refer to the
  editions of GORILA I-V"), citing shared-form signs as `AB nn (VALUE)`
  (e.g. "AB 60 (RA)") and Linear-A-only signs as bare `A nnn` (e.g. "A 664,"
  never given a phonetic reading) — the AB/A split *is* the convention this
  repo's CLAUDE.md warns about, used here unglossed.
- **Crucially, this is a preliminary narrative overview, not the full
  epigraphic edition.** It names individual signs in running prose
  face-by-face but prints no continuous line-by-line transliteration with
  the standard Leiden apparatus (brackets, dots, lacunae). The full
  sign-by-sign publication is explicitly reserved for a forthcoming volume,
  cited three times: Kanta (ed.), *forthcoming*, *Anetaki II: the
  Neopalatial Room 1 and the Ivory Repository*.

## Our assessment

Verification **V** — read in full from the primary PDF, 2026-09-12.

Because no continuous transliteration is printed, **no complete Linear A
word (2+ signs, securely read) can be extracted from this article**. Every
specific sign the authors name is cited singly, as a palaeographic parallel
or as one component of a two-sign ligature described only in prose — never
as an ordered multi-sign reading. It remains true, as before, that this
text is not in our loaded corpus and contributes no word tokens; that will
change only once the forthcoming full edition appears.

Called (by the excavator) a "religious sceptre"; the epigraphic authors
liken its calligraphic, iconography-integrated style to Cretan Hieroglyphic
seal-cutting practice more than to ordinary Linear A tablet writing, and
flag it as "a key new feature, important for ongoing palaeographical
studies" rather than as a Linear A/Cretan Hieroglyphic "bridge" claim in any
stronger sense.
