# Kober (1945), *The Cryptograms of Crete*

A worked example for a general readership, one year before the triplets paper. Read from
the two printed pages. It walks through a set of seven or eight Knossos
tablets found stacked together (Evans, *Scripta Minoa* fig. 21; her transcription and
restoration in AJA 48 (1944), 64–75).

## What it says

- **Work from the object, not the transcription.** Her transcription of the group
  "shows decided differences" from Evans's. "What a single person thought he saw in an
  inscription at one time is not necessarily what was there. The possibility that there
  is an error in a transcription must always be kept in mind." Photographs are often
  the only access, and the originals may no longer exist.
- **The numeral system, inferred from the tablets alone.** Never more than nine
  vertical strokes, so a short horizontal stroke is ten; never more than nine
  horizontals, so the circle is a hundred; the circle with four projections is a
  thousand. Read from stroke counts and positions, no value assumed.
- **The one word with a known meaning, found by position.** "The word consists of the
  first two signs on the [last] tablet, and probably means 'sum' or 'total', since it
  occurs several times at the end of a list, always with a number large enough to be
  the total of the preceding items." This is the word later read as *to-so*.
- **Word division.** "The Cretans were word-conscious": a divider stroke at the bottom
  of the line, distinct from the numeral 1 at the top; sometimes omitted where sign
  size or spacing sets a word off.
- **Recurrence across tablets.** "Certain words occur in different contexts; i.e., the
  second word in Figure 5 is the same as the third word in Figure 6. Such recurrences
  may prove very helpful in the eventual decipherment."
- **The ideogram shared by the group** (Evans's "adze") and its variants, one perhaps
  male and one female, inferred from which numbers each precedes.
- **On motive.** "The fascination of the work lies in the puzzle presented by the
  problem, rather than in the ultimate solution. An inventory that can be read is
  interesting only to an economist or to a linguist."

## What it verifies for this project

- **Her use of context was document-level and functional.** The one inference from
  context in this piece identifies a function word by its slot in a *list*: last entry,
  followed by a number that sums the rest. That is alignment of an entry to a role within
  a document, not a class a word carries independent of its document. Kober 0.4's slot
  classes (F-026) encoded the latter and found it uninformative; this piece is
  consistent with the reason given there, and it shows what the informative
  representation would be: the list as a structure with positions and totals.
- **Her caution about transcriptions** is the same concern as A-003 and A-004 here,
  and F-015's finding that the Linear A chain drops the editorial apparatus is a case
  of exactly the loss she warns of.
- **Recurrence of a word across tablets** is the raw material of the triplets: the
  same word in different contexts, compared. The 1946 paper, still unread here, is
  where this becomes inflection.

## Our assessment

The clearest short statement of her working rules: object over transcription, inference
from position and count before any value, and small sure findings. It supports F-027's
reading that what she published was a few sure identifications, not a ranked list, and
it names the kind of structure, list position with a total, that a context-aware
instrument would have to represent. It does not describe the triplet method itself.
