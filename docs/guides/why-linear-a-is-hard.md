# Why Linear A is hard

Three obstacles, each sufficient on its own. Together they explain why confident readings
keep appearing and none of them sticks.

## The circularity trap

This is the one that catches almost everybody.

Linear A signs are conventionally written out using the sound values of the Linear B signs
they resemble. So a Linear A sign shaped like the Linear B sign for *ka* is transcribed
*ka*. That convention is useful for referring to things consistently, and it is not
evidence about anything.

The trap is what happens next. If you take those transcriptions, look for words that
resemble words in some known language, and conclude that Linear A records that language,
you have used an assumption about the sound values to prove a conclusion about the sound
values. The argument is circular, and it is the single most common reason Linear A work is
dismissed.

How bad is it? Two scholars, Torsten Meißner and Philippa Steele, worked out which values
can honestly be carried across. Twenty-five signs are demonstrably shared. About sixty
more are assumed on the basis of similar shape, which is a much weaker claim. So most of
the transcription you see is convention rather than knowledge.

There is a further oddity. Seven Linear B signs for the vowel *o* have no Linear A
ancestor, and *o* is nearly absent from Linear A transcriptions, at under 3% of vowels.
Either Minoan barely used that vowel, or the values are wrong somewhere. Both are
possible and neither is established.

## The corpus is too small

Linear A runs to roughly 7,500 signs. For comparison, the machine learning systems that
successfully restore damaged Greek and Latin inscriptions were trained on 78,000 and
176,000 inscriptions respectively. Linear A is three orders of magnitude short.

Ventris succeeded on Linear B partly because he had ten times more material, and partly
because Greek was a candidate he could test against. Neither applies here.

There is a formal version of this problem. Claude Shannon defined the point past which a
coded message becomes uniquely solvable in principle, given how much redundancy it has.
A short text in an unknown language, with no parallel version in a known one, may simply
sit below that threshold. If so, no method however good will settle it, and the honest
answer is that the question is currently undecidable rather than open.

Eighty-four percent of Linear A words appear exactly once. There is very little repetition
to learn from.

## There is no bilingual

Egyptian hieroglyphs were cracked because the Rosetta Stone carried the same text in
Greek. Linear B was cracked because Ventris could guess at place names and test whether
the resulting Greek made sense.

Linear A has neither. There is no parallel text, and no known language to test against.
Every automatic decipherment that has ever worked assumed a known or candidate related
language. Linear A supplies word divisions and nothing else.

## Why confident readings keep appearing

Because matching is easy and the checks are rarely run.

If you allow yourself a language with three-consonant roots, a writing system that cannot
distinguish several sounds that language treats as different, and freedom over the vowels,
then almost any word will match something. We measured this. Under one such scheme each
Linear A word can be read as about twenty-one different Hebrew candidates, and one random
three-consonant pattern in five is a real Hebrew root before you assume anything at all.

At that point a coherent-looking translation is not evidence. Getting one is the expected
outcome whether the hypothesis is right or wrong.

The remedy has been known since 1974, when David Packard scrambled the sign values at
random and checked whether his real reading scored better than the fakes. The field had
the right instrument and stopped using it. [How we test a claim](how-we-test-a-claim.md)
describes putting it back.
